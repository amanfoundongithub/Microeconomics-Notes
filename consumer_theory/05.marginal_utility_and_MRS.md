# Marginal Rate of Substitution

Up until now, we have discussed about the consumer's preference in broad terms and in the last section, we briefly introduced the concept of marginal thinking.

In this section, we will expand upon these concepts to introduce two important concepts of the modern economics.

## Marginal Rate of Substitution - Definition

While the marginal utility is a good measure of how well a consumer models their behavior upon addition of a single good, it is essentially a _theoretic_ concept, i.e. the concept of marginal utility cannot be observed or measured by any manner.

What, however, is more observable in the real scenario is a parameter called as `marginal rate of substitution (MRS)`.

As the name suggest, **MRS is a measure at which a consumer is willing to trade one good for another to keep their utility contant**.

How does this relate to the concept of marginal utility?

To understand this, let us picture a situation with $n$ goods and a consumption bundle as $X = (x_1, x_2, ..., x_n)$.

Suppose that we change the $i^{th}$ good by $\triangle x_i$. How much change can we do in, say the $j^{th}$ good, to mantain the same utility?

To answer this question, we consider how the utilities changes with respect to the changes in the quantity of goods consumed. 

How will we measure this? This is nothing, but marginal utility!

For $i^{th}$ good, the change in utility is given by:
<p align = 'center'>
$\triangle U_i = MU_i \triangle x_i$.
</p>

Similarly, for the $j^{th}$ good, the change in utility is given by:
<p align = 'center'>
$\triangle U_j = MU_j \triangle x_j$.
</p>

Thus, to keep the utility constant, these effects need to be _equal_ and _opposite_ to each other. Thus, we get:

<p align = 'center'>
${\triangle U_i} + \triangle U_j = 0$
</p>

<p align = 'center'>
$\implies MU_i \triangle x_i + MU_j \triangle x_j = 0$
</p>

<p align = 'center'>
$\implies \triangle x_j = -\frac{MU_i}{MU_j} \triangle x_i$
</p>

or more appropriately,

<p align = 'center'>
$\frac{\triangle x_j}{\triangle x_i} = -\frac{MU_i}{MU_j}$
</p>

The ratio in the above equation represents the rate at which $x_j$ needs to be changed in accordance with $x_i$ and hence we define the MRS as follows:

<p align = 'center'>
$MRS(x_i, x_j) = \frac{\triangle x_j}{\triangle x_i} = -\frac{MU_i}{MU_j}$
</p>
